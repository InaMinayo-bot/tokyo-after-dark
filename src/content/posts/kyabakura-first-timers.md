Last checked: 14 October 2026.

A kyabakura (キャバクラ) is a room where staff sit with a small group of guests, pour drinks, and keep the conversation going for a timed visit. In English that room is usually called a hostess club. This note explains the format, the bill, and how to choose a shop yourself. It names no venues. The yen figures below are general ranges from two commercial explainers, plus one arithmetic example that is labelled fictional. They are not a quote from a door in Kabukicho, Roppongi, or Ginza.

This is general information from public sources. It is not legal advice.

## What the law is describing

The national statute is the 風営法, the Act on Control and Improvement of Amusement Business. Article 2(1)(i) covers cabaret and similar businesses that entertain guests and serve food or drink. Article 2(3) defines that entertainment, 接待 (settai), as entertaining a guest in a way that creates a party atmosphere. Article 3 requires a permit for each premises, from the prefectural public safety commission.

The National Police Agency's interpretation standard, reissued on 30 May 2025, says 接待 is more than ordinary service. Sitting with a specific small group and continuing the conversation, pouring drinks and staying, singing with them, dancing with them, or body contact counts. Serving from behind a counter and leaving promptly, or making small talk and then leaving, does not. A handshake, or helping a drunk person, is not body-contact entertainment. The standard also says this entertainment is usually done by someone of another gender, and that it is not limited to that. The shop's name does not decide the permit. What the staff actually do decides it.

A type-1 operator has to display the specified fees where guests can see them (Article 17). Giving a false or misleading explanation of those fees is prohibited (Article 18-3(i)). So is putting entertainment, food, or drink down before the guest has ordered, in a way that pressures them (Article 18-3(iii)). Under-18s may not enter a cabaret-style premises, and the operator must post that at the door (Article 18).

Licensed shops in this category are regulated businesses. Going into one is not a crime. If you are overcharged, threatened, or drugged, you are the victim. The note on [what to do when a nightlife bill is wrong](/tokyo-after-dark/posts/tokyo-nightlife-scams/) is linked again at the end of this page.

## How the other rooms differ

People use several English names for rooms that look similar from the street. The legal test above is the one the police publish. The rest of this section is the practical picture that goes with it.

**Hostess club.** In English this usually means the same room as a kyabakura: staff sit at the table for a set length of time, and the bill is built from that time, any nomination, and drinks.

**Girls bar.** Staff usually work from behind the counter. They take an order, may chat, and leave. On the National Police Agency test, that is ordinary service. If the same staff sit down with a small group and keep entertaining them, the visit can become 接待, whatever the sign says.

**Snack bar (スナック).** A snack is a smaller room, often run by a mama, with regulars and karaoke. The same test applies. Sitting with a specific group, pouring, and staying is 接待. Serving and leaving is not.

**Lounge or club in Ginza.** When staff sit with the table and entertain, the room is in the same legal family. Industry price writing puts high-end city districts at the top of the set-charge band quoted below. No official Ginza price table was found for this guide, and this page does not treat a membership rule or a bottle-keep period as established fact.

**Host club.** Hosts sit with the table. The National Police Agency standard does not limit 接待 to one gender. The Tokyo Metropolitan Comprehensive Consumer Affairs Center's warning of 18 December 2024, about missing itemisation, running tabs, and pressure to pay, is about host clubs and concept cafes. It is not a kyabakura fee schedule.

## How the bill works

Ask for the price list before you sit. Article 17 is a display duty for specified fees, and Article 18-3 forbids a misleading explanation of them. A number said out loud is a weaker promise than a posted one: the National Tax Agency's total-price rule (Consumption Tax Act, Article 63) requires a price displayed in advance to consumers to include consumption tax, and it says a spoken price is outside that duty. A sign that reads "10,000 yen" is supposed to be the tax-included figure. "About 8,000" at the door may leave the service charge and the tax for later. Ask whether both are already in the number.

The lines that show up on explainers' sample bills are these.

**Set, or set time.** A charge for a block of time. [KYABA.jp](https://kyaba.jp/guide/price/) describes it as the seat plus a house bottle for the guest, with drinks for the cast and any nomination on top. [Kyabakura Web](https://kyabakura-web.com/asobitai/kyabakura-ryokin/) describes a house pour as often included, and drinks for the cast as extra. The length is on the list. KYABA.jp's examples use 60 minutes. Kyabakura Web's examples use about 40 to 60 minutes.

**Extension (延長, enchō).** Staying past the block. KYABA.jp says the extension is often about another set charge, plus service on that charge, and that some shops extend with no one asking you. Kyabakura Web puts a common extension at about 3,000 yen for 30 minutes or about 5,000 yen for 60 minutes, and it also says some shops extend automatically. Ask which kind of shop you are in before you sit.

**Nomination (指名, shimei).** Optional. KYABA.jp's glossary says フリー (furī) means you do not nominate: the shop seats someone, and there is no nomination fee. 場内指名 (jōnai shimei) is choosing someone after you have sat down. 本指名 (hon shimei) is asking for a particular person from the start. KYABA.jp says the in-house choice is often the cheaper of the two.

**Drinks for the cast.** A separate charge, per glass, and it is easy for the number of glasses to climb. Your own house pour is the part the set explainers treat as included.

**Bottles.** Optional, and often a much larger line. KYABA.jp says the bottle price can have ice and water added, and that some shops charge to keep what is left. No keep period was found in the sources read for this guide. Ask the price before a bottle is opened. You can decline.

**Service charge and tax.** On a nightlife slip, "TAX" or サービス料 (sābisu-ryō) is often a service percentage, and consumption tax may be inside that line or added after it. KYABA.jp says a service charge of about 20 percent of the subtotal is commonly cited, and that shops differ on whether "TAX" already includes consumption tax. Kyabakura Web says, in one passage, that TAX means consumption tax plus service and that about 15 to 20 percent is added, and later on the same page that service is generally 20 to 35 percent with 10 percent consumption tax on top. Those two sentences do not match. Use the list in front of you, and ask which method that shop uses.

**Other lines that are not automatic.** Some shops add a charge for a guest who comes alone. Kyabakura Web gives about 1,000 to 3,000 yen for shops that do this, and it says some shops do not charge it. Some shops add a card fee. Kyabakura Web gives about 5 to 10 percent where a fee exists. KYABA.jp says to check the payment method first, because some shops are cash only. A late-night surcharge, a companion fee for meeting before the shop opens (同伴, dōhan), and a private-room fee are separate. None of them is part of simply sitting down. Ask, or decline.

### Ranges the explainers actually publish

There is no official price table for Kabukicho, Roppongi, or Ginza in the sources read for this guide. Single-shop menus were not used. What follows is what the two explainers say, including where they disagree.

KYABA.jp, updated 23 September 2026, repeats an industry band and labels it as not the site's own census. For a 60-minute set: about 5,000 to 10,000 yen as the commonly quoted middle; 15,000 to 30,000 yen or more in high-end districts of large cities; and, in some regional cities, about 3,000 to 5,000 yen. Drinks about 1,000 yen a glass. 場内指名 about 1,000 yen. 本指名 about 3,000 yen. Service about 20 percent of the subtotal.

Kyabakura Web, updated 18 May 2026, describes a set of about 5,000 to 8,000 yen for roughly 40 to 60 minutes, and says city centres tend to cost more than suburbs, where a set can be under 5,000 yen. Cast drinks and food about 1,000 to 3,000 yen. Bottles from about 5,000 yen upward. 場内指名 about 1,000 to 3,000 yen. 本指名 about 2,000 to 3,000 yen. Extension about 3,000 yen for 30 minutes or 5,000 yen for 60. Service, as noted above, is described two different ways on that page.

The number that matters is the list at that door, read before you sit.

These figures are the sample numbers in KYABA.jp's price calculator and in the illustration next to it. They are not a bill from a Tokyo shop. Shops also differ on whether tax is applied after the service charge, and on rounding. KYABA.jp's prose rounds the same illustration to about 14,500 yen. - Service charge at 20 percent: 2,200 yen
- Consumption tax at 10 percent of 13,200 yen: 1,320 yen
- Total: 14,520 yen

If the visit runs into a second set, KYABA.jp says to budget another set charge and the service on it. That second block is not included here.

## Hours

Article 13 says a 風俗営業 must not operate in the late-night window, midnight to 6:00 a.m., unless a prefectural ordinance allows a later hour in designated areas. The Tokyo Public Safety Commission rule on the ordinance sets that extended hour at 1:00 a.m. for the areas the rule covers (Article 6). That is not a statement that every kyabakura may stay open until 1:00 a.m. KYABA.jp's September 2026 snapshot of 4,461 shops with hours data found 1:00 a.m. the most common closing time (50.1 percent) and midnight the next (21.9 percent). Some listings ran later, and KYABA.jp notes that a later closing can be a different kind of permit. Ask the shop.

## Etiquette

The visit is conversation over drinks, for the time you agreed. Nomination is a choice. Drinks for the cast are a choice. A bottle is a choice.

No statute read for this guide sets a customer touching rule, a photo ban, or a tip rate. The National Police Agency standard treats body contact as something the business side may be doing as 接待. That is a permit test. It is not permission for a guest to touch staff. If you are asked to stop, stop.

KYABA.jp's glossary says exchanging contact details is optional, and that choosing not to exchange them is a normal outcome. Photos of staff are a house rule: ask before you raise a phone. None of the price explainers read here list a tip. Japan does not add a tip line to this bill. The charge is the set, any nomination, the drinks, the service charge, and tax.

Both explainers tell a first-timer to decide a ceiling before sitting: how many extensions, how many cast drinks, and whether to nominate. Kyabakura Web also suggests telling the floor staff the budget and the leaving time, so the table is not still open when you wanted to stop. Declining is ordinary. The phrases below are a polite way to do it.

## Foreign guests

No provision in the 風営法 articles read for this guide says a licensed shop may admit only Japanese customers. Some shops still refuse foreign guests, or seat only people who come with an introduction. That is a house rule, not a line in the statute. Ask before you commit. If the answer is no, leave. A tout who promises a shop that "welcomes foreigners" is a reason to walk away. Licensed shops operate openly, so you can choose one yourself. The scam guide covers touts in more detail.

Japanese is the usual language at the table. Ask whether anyone speaks English, and do not treat a yes as a promise that the whole visit will be in English.

Under-18s may not enter (Article 18). Shops commonly ask for photo ID to check that. The Metropolitan Police visitor page tells foreign nationals to carry their passport. Showing it is for a real police officer who asks. This guide did not re-read the statute on the legal drinking age, so it does not state that age here.

## Choosing a shop

Pick the shop yourself. Look it up, read recent accounts, and ask the hotel desk or someone who lives in the city if you want a second opinion. Street touts (客引き, kyakuhiki) are the pattern the Metropolitan Police and the embassies warn about. Article 7 of the Tokyo ordinance against public nuisance restricts several kinds of street solicitation, including touting for certain hospitality over drinks. The scam guide has the citation and the penalty.

The Metropolitan Police describe a common lie: the tout stands outside a known bar, says it is full, and offers a "sister shop." The second place is unrelated. Keep walking.

Before you sit, read the list for five things: the length of the set, the set charge, the service charge and whether tax is included, the nomination fees, and the price of a drink for the cast. Then ask two questions: does the time extend only after someone checks with you, and may a single guest come in, and at what extra charge.

Red flags are a missing price list, a refusal to say whether service and tax are included, an automatic extension you were not told about, pressure to open a bottle, a card taken where you cannot see it, and drinks put down before you have ordered. Article 18-3 is the statute behind the false-fee point and the unordered-drink point.

## If the bill is wrong

Do not sign or pay under pressure, and keep any receipt you already have. What to do next — police on 110 or at a kōban, the Japan Visitor Hotline, the card issuer, and the consumer centre — is written out in [Tokyo Nightlife Scams: How to Spot Them and What to Do if It Happens to You](/tokyo-after-dark/posts/tokyo-nightlife-scams/). If you are overcharged, threatened, or drugged, you are the victim. Report it.

## FAQ

### Is a kyabakura the same thing as a hostess club?

In English, yes: both names usually mean the timed table with staff sitting down. Girls bars, snack bars, Ginza lounges, and host clubs are the other rooms people mix up with it. The police test is whether staff sit and keep entertaining, not what the sign says.

### Do I have to nominate someone?

No. フリー is a visit with no nomination fee. 場内指名 and 本指名 are optional, and the in-house choice is often cheaper. Confirm that on the list, because some shops add the fee once per set.

### What if the clock extends on its own?

Some shops do that. Ask "自動延長ですか" before you sit, and ask them to check with you first. Watch the time yourself.

### Can I go alone?

Ask at the door. Some shops add a charge for one guest, and Kyabakura Web's range for shops that do is about 1,000 to 3,000 yen. Some shops do not charge it. KYABA.jp's review sample mentions people going alone; it does not publish a standard solo fee.

### What ID do I need?

Under-18s may not enter, and shops commonly check photo ID. Foreign nationals should carry a passport, as the Metropolitan Police visitor page says. This guide does not state a drinking age.

### What if I do not speak Japanese?

Ask "外国人でも入れますか" and "英語は話せますか" before you commit. Some shops are Japanese-only as a house rule. If the answer is no, leave. Do not follow a tout to a place that promises otherwise.

### Is touching expected?

No. The visit is conversation over drinks. If you are asked to stop, stop. "触るのはやめてください" means "please stop touching."

### Can I take photos or exchange LINE?

Ask. No statute read for this guide bans photos or requires a contact exchange. KYABA.jp's glossary treats exchanging details as optional. Declining is a complete answer.

### Do I tip?

No tip rate was found. The explainers' bills are the set, nomination, drinks, service, and tax. Do not add a percentage on top.

### What if the bill is wrong?

Keep the receipt, and do not pay under pressure. Use the [scam guide](/tokyo-after-dark/posts/tokyo-nightlife-scams/) for 110, a kōban, the visitor hotline, the card issuer, and the consumer centre.
