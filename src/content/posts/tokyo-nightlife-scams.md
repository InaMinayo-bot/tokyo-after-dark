Last checked: 14 October 2026.

Tokyo is a safe city to walk around. The risk that embassies and the Tokyo Metropolitan Police describe is narrower than that. It sits in entertainment districts at night: a stranger offers you a bar, the bill is nothing like the price you heard, or you wake up to card charges you do not remember making. This note is general information from public sources. It is not legal advice for a particular case, and it does not name venues.

The areas named most often are Kabukicho in Shinjuku, Roppongi, Shibuya, and Ikebukuro. The US State Department, the UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office, and Australia’s Smartraveller all list those districts. The Metropolitan Police also warn about touts and rip-offs in Kinshicho, Shimbashi, Ueno, and Tachikawa. The same pattern shows up in Osaka, but this note stays with Tokyo.

## You do not need anyone to take you there

Licensed adult entertainment shops (風俗, fuuzoku) are legal, licensed businesses in Japan. They are regulated under the 風営法, the Act on Control and Improvement of Amusement Business. Going to one is not a crime.

If you are overcharged, threatened, or drugged, you are the victim. Report it without worrying. Call 110, or walk into the nearest police box (交番, a koban).

Licensed shops operate openly, so you never need to follow a tout. Look the place up yourself, read recent accounts, ask the hotel desk or someone who lives in the city, and check the prices before you sit down. Police warnings also mention illegal gambling rooms, and shops that do not do what a tout promised.

## Street touts

A street tout is 客引き (kyakuhiki): someone who stops passers-by and tries to walk them into a bar, a hostess club, or another shop. The Metropolitan Police say malicious touting can breach both the amusement-business law and the Tokyo ordinance against public nuisance (東京都迷惑防止条例). Article 7 of that ordinance bans several kinds of street solicitation. They include touting for sexualised shows or services, touting for certain kinds of hospitality over drinks, and persistent touting — grabbing clothing, blocking the path, or following someone. A breach of that ban can mean a fine of up to 500,000 yen, or detention or a petty fine. Paying someone else to do the touting is a separate offence.

Police describe a common lie: the tout stands outside a known bar, tells a person who already has a booking that the room is full, and offers a “sister shop.” The second place is unrelated, and it is often a rip-off bar. The UK’s travel advice says most British victims who reported this pattern were approached in English in Kabukicho. The advice from both the police and the UK government is the same: do not follow a tout into a bar.

## Inflated bills

ぼったくり (bottakuri) is the word people use for a bill that has been inflated. The Metropolitan Police say touts take people to a partner bar and, once the guest is drunk, charge excessive fees or use a credit card without the guest’s knowledge. In some accounts the first drink is very strong, the person becomes dazed, and the tab or the card charges appear only afterwards. The UK government says travellers have been presented with bills far beyond what they ordered, and that some British nationals were arrested after a dispute with staff or door staff over a bill. See a menu with prices before you go in. Keep track of what you order. Ask the admission or table charge at the door, before you sit.

The Tokyo Metropolitan Comprehensive Consumer Affairs Center describes a related problem at some host clubs and concept cafes: a high bill with no breakdown, a running tab, and pressure to pay. Its advice is to get the details in writing and to talk to a consumer advice office or the police if the demand turns threatening.

## Drink spiking and the card charges that follow

The US State Department, the UK government, and the Australian Embassy in Tokyo all warn about drinks being spiked in nightlife areas, especially Kabukicho, Roppongi, Shibuya, and Ikebukuro. Victims describe one drink, then a gap in memory. Some wake in the bar. Others wake on the street or somewhere they do not recognise. Card charges, missing cash, or a trip to an ATM can fall inside that gap. The UK government says reported losses have ranged from hundreds of pounds to tens of thousands. The Metropolitan Police describe the same sequence at bars a tout recommended, including in Shimbashi and Ueno: a spiked drink, then an ATM withdrawal to “settle” a bill.

Smartraveller says both men and women have been targeted, and that some drinks are stronger than they appear. If you feel suddenly unwell, tell the people you are with and get medical help. Do not accept a drink from someone you just met. Do not leave a drink and come back to it.

The UK government adds a warning that is easy to miss. If police find an illegal drug in your system, you can be charged even if you say you did not consent to taking it. Refusing a test can itself lead to arrest. If you think a drink was spiked, contact the police and your embassy rather than trying to handle it inside the bar.

The US State Department also reports threats with a weapon, and assaults, when people refused to pay an excessive bill or to withdraw cash. If that is happening, call 110. Do not stay and negotiate.

## “Free drinks” and all-you-can-drink offers

The UK government says touts get people through the door by misdescribing the place, often with a promise of a free drink or “just a look.” There can be an entry charge, and you may not be free to leave. Treat an all-you-can-drink offer — 飲み放題, nomihoudai — from someone who stopped you on the street the same way. A bar that wants your business can show you a price list at the door.

## Hostess clubs, girls bars, and host clubs

At a hostess club, the ordinary bill is a block of time, a nomination fee if you asked for a particular person, and drinks. Drinks for the staff at the table are a normal part of that system when they are explained before you sit. The Metropolitan Police list hostess bars and girls bars among the places illegal touts push, and they say the promised service is often not what you get, or the bill is inexplicably large. That is the trap: the price you were told at the kerb is not the price on the slip, the time is extended without a clear question, or nobody will show you how the total was built.

The consumer centre’s published warning about missing itemisation and aggressive collection is about host clubs and concept cafes, including people drawn in through social media or a dating app. The same habits protect you in a hostess club or a girls bar. Ask for the time charge, the nomination fee, the drink prices, and the staff-drink rule before you order. If the answer is vague, leave.

## Cards used without your knowledge

Police and embassies describe cards used while someone is drunk or unconscious, and fraudulent charges at restaurants and bars a tout chose. In Kinshicho, the Metropolitan Police have warned about fraudulent card charges at those spots since around 2018. Watch the terminal. Do not hand a card over and look away. Smartraveller says not to carry large amounts of cash, and to be careful with your card in bars and clubs. It also suggests turning off face or fingerprint unlock on your phone, so someone who has the phone cannot open a banking app with it.

These reports describe charges you did not authorise. They do not, on the official pages, describe a particular skimming device.

## Someone pretending to be the police

A real police officer can ask to see your passport. Foreign nationals are required to carry it and to present it when an officer asks. An identity check is not a request for cash, your PIN, or a trip to an ATM.

Separately, Inagi City, citing the way these calls work, warns about people who impersonate police by phone or video call. They talk about a bank account “used in a crime,” show a badge or a warrant on screen, move the conversation to a messaging app, and ask for a transfer. The city’s page says officers do not demand money, do not contact you on social media for this, and do not send pictures of badges or warrants. Hang up and call #9110, or the police station number you looked up yourself.

## A stranger who is suddenly very friendly

The Metropolitan Police describe two versions. On the street, someone — sometimes a tout who is not Japanese, sometimes a woman who approaches a man — insists on a particular bar. On a dating app or social media, a person you have only just met says they know a good place. After a few drinks they disappear, and the bill is enormous. The police page on Kabukicho also describes a “badger game”: a promise of paid sex, then other people demanding money or using the situation as blackmail. Do not go to a second location with someone you have just met. Do not pay a “deposit” to someone on the street.

## If it happens

1. If you are being threatened, or you cannot safely leave, call 110. You can also walk into the nearest koban. For a non-emergency question, #9110 is the police consultation line, and the Metropolitan Police say English is available. Few officers speak English, so the US State Department suggests being able to show where you are on a map.
2. Do not sign a blank slip, a bill you cannot read, or a card receipt you have not checked. The UK government reports arrests after arguments with staff over excessive bills, so call the police rather than fighting the tab at the table.
3. If you can leave, write down the shop’s name, the address, the time, and what you were told the price would be. Keep receipts and photograph the bill.
4. If a card was used, call the issuer and dispute the charges. File a police report before you leave Japan. The US State Department says Japanese police usually will not take a report from overseas, and that they often give a report number rather than a long statement. Give that number to the card company.
5. Call your embassy or consulate. The US, UK, and Australian governments all tell their nationals to report these crimes. An embassy cannot investigate for you. It can help you contact police and explain what documents a card dispute or an insurance claim needs.
6. Call the Japan Visitor Hotline, 050-3816-2787 (from overseas, +81-50-3816-2787). The Japan National Tourism Organization runs it 24 hours a day, every day, in English, Chinese, Korean, and Japanese. It handles tourist information and help in an accident, illness, or disaster. It is not a replacement for 110.
7. For a billing dispute, use a consumer advice office. The consumer hotline is 188. It connects you to a local centre. The call is not free; the consultation is. If 188 is busy, the National Consumer Affairs Center’s backup line is 03-3446-0999 on weekdays from 10:00 to 16:00. The Tokyo Metropolitan Comprehensive Consumer Affairs Center is 03-3235-1155, Monday to Saturday, 9:00 to 17:00, closed Sundays, national holidays, and 29 December to 3 January. Advice there is free. English, Chinese, Korean, Tagalog, and Vietnamese are available through an interpreter. That Tokyo line is for people who live, work, or study in Tokyo. A short-stay visitor who does not should start with 188 or the Japan Visitor Hotline.

## Before you go out

- Pick the bar yourself. Do not follow a tout, a scout, or someone you met five minutes ago.
- Read the prices, including any table charge or time charge, before you sit down. If nobody will tell you, leave.
- At a hostess club, ask about the time block, the nomination fee, and drinks for staff before you order.
- Do not go in on the promise of a free drink or a look around.
- Carry one card and the cash you are willing to spend. Leave the rest at the hotel.
- Keep the drink you are holding in sight. Do not accept one from a stranger.
- Go with people you trust, and agree a way to leave.
- If a friend becomes confused or unusually drowsy, stay with them and get medical help.
- Do not sign a bill you have not read. Watch the card terminal.
- If someone claiming to be police asks for money, hang up or walk to a koban. Show your passport to an actual officer when asked.
- Save 110, #9110, and 050-3816-2787 in your phone before you need them.
